# 4. Discussion

## 4.1 Summary of Main Findings

This cross-sectional study investigated retinal structural changes in 164 MDD patients (325 eyes) and 87 healthy controls (174 eyes) using comprehensive OCT analysis. Primary analyses were conducted at the eye level with participant ID as a random effect to account for inter-eye correlation. The principal findings are: (1) MDD patients exhibited significant retinal thinning, particularly in the macular outer temporal region; (2) optic disc analysis revealed increased cup-to-disc ratio and decreased rim volume, suggesting structural changes associated with glaucomatous optic neuropathy that warrant further ophthalmologic evaluation; (3) these associations remained significant after controlling for age and sex, indicating an independent relationship between depression and retinal alterations; (4) OCT parameters showed limited diagnostic value for distinguishing MDD patients from controls; and (5) unexpectedly, higher depression severity was associated with greater thickness in certain macular regions, suggesting complex relationships that warrant longitudinal investigation.

## 4.2 Interpretation of Macular Changes

The most prominent finding was the significant reduction in macular thickness in MDD patients, with the outer temporal region showing the largest effect size (Cohen's d=-0.50). This region corresponds to the papillomacular bundle, which contains axons from ganglion cells that project to the lateral geniculate nucleus and subsequently to the primary visual cortex. The selective vulnerability of this region may reflect its high metabolic demand and susceptibility to neuroinflammatory processes that have been implicated in depression pathophysiology.

The observation that changes were most pronounced in the Retina layer (full-thickness retina) rather than specific neuronal layers (GC-IPL or GCC) suggests that the alterations may involve multiple retinal components, including glial cells and vasculature, rather than purely neuronal loss. This is consistent with the neuroinflammatory hypothesis of depression, which posits that activated microglia and astrocytes contribute to both central and retinal pathology (Miller and Raison, 2016).

The lack of significant changes in the choroid layer is noteworthy. Given that choroidal thickness reflects vascular perfusion, this finding suggests that the observed retinal changes are not primarily driven by macrovascular alterations, but rather by neurodegenerative or microvascular processes affecting the inner retinal layers.

## 4.3 Optic Disc Changes and Clinical Implications

The finding of increased cup-to-disc ratio and decreased rim volume in MDD patients suggests optic disc structural changes that require further ophthalmologic evaluation to determine their clinical significance. Several mechanisms could explain this association:

First, depression has been associated with autonomic dysregulation, including impaired blood flow autoregulation. The optic nerve head is particularly vulnerable to perfusion fluctuations, and chronic autonomic dysfunction could lead to remodeling of the optic disc architecture.

Second, both depression and glaucoma have been linked to mitochondrial dysfunction and oxidative stress (Maes et al., 2011; Bansal and Kuhad, 2016). Shared pathophysiological pathways could lead to convergent structural changes in the optic nerve.

Third, antidepressant medications, particularly selective serotonin reuptake inhibitors (SSRIs), have been associated with increased risk of angle-closure glaucoma in susceptible individuals. However, in our study, the structural changes were observed in the context of open-angle configuration (normal disc area), suggesting a different mechanism than angle closure.

**However, we emphasize that our study was not designed to diagnose glaucoma or establish glaucoma-MDD comorbidity.** The observed changes in cup-to-disc ratio and rim volume, while statistically significant, fall within the range of normal variation and do not meet criteria for glaucomatous optic neuropathy without corroborating evidence of functional damage (visual field defects) or elevated intraocular pressure. These findings should be interpreted as suggestive of potential structural vulnerability rather than definitive evidence of glaucomatous disease. Future studies incorporating comprehensive glaucoma screening (automated perimetry, tonometry, pachymetry) are needed to clarify the clinical significance of these observations.

## 4.4 Relationship with Depression Severity

The unexpected positive correlation between depression severity and macular thickness in certain regions suggests that the relationship between depression and retinal structure may be more complex than simple cumulative neurodegeneration. 

Several factors may explain this finding. First, the presence of patients with minimal symptoms (PHQ-9 < 5, 39.6% of assessed eyes) reflects the heterogeneous nature of our MDD sample, which included patients in remission or receiving effective treatment. These patients maintained their MDD diagnosis but had controlled symptoms at the time of assessment, representing the natural course of depressive disorders where diagnosis persists beyond acute symptom episodes.

Second, alternative biological explanations include inflammatory processes, medication effects, or stage-dependent changes that require longitudinal studies to distinguish. The cross-sectional design limits our ability to determine whether the observed associations represent trait markers of vulnerability or state markers of active illness. Several explanations should be considered:

**Inflammatory edema**: Higher depression severity may be associated with more pronounced neuroinflammation, which could initially present as retinal edema rather than atrophy. In this scenario, increased thickness would represent an early pathological change that may eventually progress to thinning as the disease course evolves.

**Compensatory mechanisms**: The retina may exhibit compensatory responses to chronic stress, including gliosis and vascular remodeling, which could transiently increase thickness before eventual failure of these compensatory mechanisms leads to atrophy.

**Treatment effects**: Patients with more severe depression may have received different treatment regimens, and some antidepressants or adjunctive medications could affect retinal structure. However, we did not find significant differences in thickness between medicated and unmedicated patients in exploratory analyses (data not shown).

**Heterogeneity of depression**: Depression is a heterogeneous disorder with potentially distinct biological subtypes. The relationship between retinal structure and symptom severity may differ across subtypes characterized by varying degrees of inflammatory versus neurodegenerative pathology.

Longitudinal studies are needed to clarify the temporal dynamics of retinal changes and their relationship to depression course and treatment response.

## 4.5 Comparison with Previous Studies

Our findings both confirm and extend previous research on retinal changes in depression. Consistent with Li et al. (2021) and Kesebir et al. (2016), we observed significant reductions in peripapillary RNFL thickness in MDD patients. However, our study extends these findings by demonstrating that macular changes are even more pronounced than optic nerve changes, with larger effect sizes observed in the macular outer temporal region compared to peripapillary RNFL.

In contrast to some previous studies that reported correlations between RNFL thickness and depression severity (Celik et al., 2016), we found weak and inconsistent associations. This discrepancy may reflect differences in study populations, depression subtypes, or measurement methodologies. Our comprehensive analysis of multiple retinal layers and regions provides a more nuanced picture of the relationship between retinal structure and depression.

The observation of optic disc structural changes in MDD patients has not been previously reported and represents a novel finding that warrants replication with comprehensive ophthalmologic evaluation.

## 4.6 Diagnostic Utility and Biomarker Potential

Despite statistically significant differences between groups, the diagnostic performance of individual OCT parameters was modest (AUC<0.70), indicating limited clinical utility as standalone diagnostic tools. This finding is consistent with the heterogeneous nature of depression and the multifactorial etiology of retinal changes.

However, several considerations suggest that OCT may still have value as a complementary biomarker:

**Stratification tool**: While insufficient for diagnosis, OCT parameters could help stratify patients by biological subtype, potentially predicting treatment response to anti-inflammatory or neuroprotective interventions.

**Monitoring tool**: Longitudinal OCT measurements could track disease progression or treatment response, particularly if the observed structural changes prove to be dynamic and reversible.

**Combination biomarker**: When combined with other biological measures (e.g., inflammatory markers, neuroimaging), OCT parameters may contribute to a multi-modal biomarker panel with improved diagnostic accuracy.

Machine learning approaches to combine multiple OCT parameters did not substantially improve diagnostic performance in our study, likely due to the modest effect sizes and high inter-parameter correlations. Future studies with larger sample sizes and inclusion of additional clinical variables may achieve better performance.

## 4.7 Strengths and Limitations

### Strengths

This study has several notable strengths. First, the sample size (251 participants, 499 eyes) is among the largest in this field, providing adequate statistical power to detect moderate effect sizes. Primary analyses were conducted at the eye level to maximize statistical efficiency, with participant ID included as a random effect to account for inter-eye correlation. Second, the comprehensive OCT analysis including five retinal layers and optic disc parameters offers a more complete picture than previous studies focusing exclusively on RNFL. Third, the inclusion of multivariate regression analysis controlling for confounding factors strengthens the validity of our findings. Fourth, the high inter-eye consistency (ICC=0.806) demonstrates the reliability of our measurements.

### Limitations

Several limitations should be acknowledged. 

**First, PHQ-9 data were unavailable for 19.5% of MDD patients** due to the timing of protocol amendment. However, analyses comparing patients with and without PHQ-9 data revealed no systematic differences in demographic or OCT characteristics, suggesting that missing data did not introduce selection bias. 

**Second, the cross-sectional design** precludes determination of causality and temporal relationships (Rothman et al., 2008; Vandenbroucke et al., 2007). We cannot determine whether retinal changes precede depression onset, result from the disorder, or represent a common risk factor. 

**Third, the inclusion of patients with minimal current symptoms** (PHQ-9 < 5) reflects real-world clinical practice where MDD diagnosis persists beyond acute episodes, but this heterogeneity may have attenuated associations between symptom severity and retinal structure. Future studies should stratify analyses by illness phase (acute vs. remission) to clarify whether retinal changes represent trait or state markers.

**Fourth, age differences** between MDD patients and controls represent another limitation. Although we controlled for age in multivariate analyses, the MDD group was significantly older [median (IQR): 38.0 (28.0-50.0) vs. 32.0 (25.0-44.0) years]. To address this concern, we conducted sensitivity analyses: (1) excluding participants >60 years (396 eyes from 198 participants), (2) stratified analysis by age groups, and (3) age-matched subsample analysis. In all analyses, the primary findings—reduced macular thickness and altered optic disc parameters in MDD patients—remained consistent with the main results, supporting the robustness of our findings despite baseline age differences.

**Fifth**, the study population was recruited from a single center, potentially limiting generalizability. 

**Sixth**, medication effects represent a potential confound. While we excluded patients taking medications known to affect retinal structure, the effects of antidepressants on retinal morphology are not fully understood. We did not have detailed information on medication duration and dosage for all patients.

**Seventh**, the lack of correlation between retinal changes and depression severity in most parameters, combined with the unexpected positive correlation in some regions, complicates the interpretation of our findings. These inconsistencies may reflect measurement variability, true biological heterogeneity, or limitations of the PHQ-9 as a measure of depression severity.

**Finally**, the diagnostic utility of OCT parameters was limited, suggesting that retinal imaging alone is insufficient for clinical diagnosis of depression.

## 4.8 Future Directions

Several directions for future research emerge from our findings:

**Longitudinal studies** are essential to determine the temporal dynamics of retinal changes, their relationship to depression course and treatment response, and whether they represent trait or state markers (Rush et al., 2006; Harmer et al., 2017).

**Mechanistic studies** using animal models could elucidate the biological pathways linking depression and retinal pathology, including the role of neuroinflammation, neurotrophic factors, and autonomic dysfunction.

**Multi-modal biomarker research** combining OCT with other measures (neuroimaging, peripheral inflammatory markers, genetic risk scores) may achieve better diagnostic and prognostic performance than any single modality.

**Intervention studies** could determine whether successful antidepressant treatment normalizes retinal structure, which would support the utility of OCT as a treatment monitoring tool.

**Subtype-specific analyses** may reveal distinct patterns of retinal changes associated with specific depression subtypes (e.g., melancholic vs. atypical, inflammatory vs. non-inflammatory).

---

# 5. Conclusion

This study demonstrates that major depressive disorder is associated with systematic structural changes in the retina, characterized by reduced macular thickness (particularly in the outer temporal region) and optic disc alterations associated with glaucomatous optic neuropathy that require further ophthalmologic evaluation. These associations remain significant after controlling for demographic confounders, supporting an independent relationship between depression and retinal pathology. However, the modest effect sizes and limited diagnostic utility suggest that OCT parameters are not suitable as standalone diagnostic biomarkers. The unexpected positive correlation between depression severity and retinal thickness in certain regions challenges simple neurodegenerative models and may reflect complex interactions between inflammatory and compensatory processes. Longitudinal studies are needed to clarify the temporal dynamics of these changes and their potential utility for treatment monitoring and biological stratification of depression.
