# Title

Retinal structural changes in patients with major depressive disorder: A cross-sectional study using optical coherence tomography

---

# Abstract

**Background**: Major depressive disorder (MDD) is associated with structural brain alterations, but its effects on the retina remain unclear. The retina shares embryological origins with the central nervous system and can be non-invasively imaged using optical coherence tomography (OCT). This study aimed to systematically investigate retinal structural changes in MDD patients.

**Methods**: We conducted a cross-sectional case-control study including 251 participants (164 patients with major depressive disorder [MDD] and 87 healthy controls). OCT scans were obtained from 499 eyes (325 MDD eyes and 174 control eyes). Primary analyses were conducted at the eye level to maximize statistical power, with participant ID included as a random effect to account for inter-eye correlation. All participants underwent comprehensive OCT imaging including macular (5-layer analysis) and optic disc parameters. Depression severity was assessed using the Patient Health Questionnaire-9 (PHQ-9). Group comparisons were performed using Mann-Whitney U tests with false discovery rate correction. Multivariate linear regression was used to control for confounding factors.

**Results**: MDD patients showed significantly reduced macular thickness compared to controls (271.45±16.91 vs 278.19±14.89 μm, P<0.001, Cohen's d=-0.42). The outer temporal region showed the largest effect size (d=-0.50, P<0.001). Optic disc analysis revealed increased cup-to-disc ratio (0.30±0.19 vs 0.25±0.18, P=0.008) and decreased rim volume in MDD patients. After controlling for age and sex, depression remained independently associated with reduced macular thickness (β=-5.67, P=0.009). However, OCT parameters showed limited diagnostic value (AUC=0.65) and weak correlation with depression severity.

**Conclusions**: MDD is associated with systematic retinal structural changes, particularly in the outer temporal macula. These findings suggest potential retinal biomarkers for MDD, though their clinical utility requires further validation in longitudinal studies.

**Keywords**: Major depressive disorder; Optical coherence tomography; Retina; Macula; Optic disc; Biomarker

# 1. Introduction

Major depressive disorder (MDD) is a leading cause of disability worldwide, affecting more than 300 million people globally (World Health Organization, 2021). Despite its high prevalence and substantial disease burden, the pathophysiology of MDD remains incompletely understood. Current diagnostic approaches rely primarily on clinical interviews and symptom-based criteria, lacking objective biological markers. The identification of reliable biomarkers could facilitate early diagnosis, enable personalized treatment selection, and improve monitoring of treatment response.

Emerging evidence suggests that MDD is associated with structural and functional alterations in the central nervous system (CNS). Neuroimaging studies have consistently demonstrated reduced gray matter volume in the prefrontal cortex, hippocampus, and anterior cingulate cortex in MDD patients (Koolschijn et al., 2009; Schmaal et al., 2017). These structural changes are thought to reflect neuroinflammation, reduced neurogenesis, and altered synaptic plasticity (Duman and Aghajanian, 2012; Miller and Raison, 2016; Duman and Monteggia, 2006).

The retina offers a unique window into the CNS due to its shared embryological origins with the brain. As an extension of the diencephalon, the retina contains similar neuronal and glial cell types and is subject to similar pathological processes affecting the brain (London et al., 2013). Moreover, the retina can be non-invasively visualized in vivo with high resolution using optical coherence tomography (OCT), making it an attractive target for biomarker discovery (Drexler and Fujimoto, 2008).

Several studies have investigated retinal changes in MDD using OCT, with most reporting reduced peripapillary retinal nerve fiber layer (RNFL) thickness in depressed patients (Li et al., 2021; Kesebir et al., 2016; Kirbas et al., 2013; Yavuz et al., 2017). However, these studies have several limitations. First, most focused exclusively on RNFL measurements, neglecting the macular region which contains the highest density of neurons. Second, few studies have performed layer-specific analysis of the macula, which may provide more sensitive markers of neuronal integrity. Third, the relationship between retinal changes and depression severity remains unclear, with conflicting results across studies. Finally, most studies have not adequately controlled for potential confounding factors such as age and sex.

The primary objective of this study was to systematically investigate retinal structural changes in MDD patients using comprehensive OCT analysis, including:
(1) Five-layer analysis of macular thickness;
(2) Optic disc parameters including RNFL and structural measures;
(3) Assessment of the relationship between retinal changes and depression severity;
(4) Evaluation of diagnostic performance of OCT parameters;
(5) Multivariate analysis controlling for confounding factors.

We hypothesized that MDD patients would show significant retinal thinning compared to healthy controls, particularly in the macular region, and that these changes would correlate with depression severity.

# 2. Methods

## 2.1 Study Design and Participants

This cross-sectional case-control study was conducted at the Third Affiliated Hospital of Sun Yat-sen University between January 2023 and December 2024. The study protocol was approved by the Institutional Review Board of the Third Affiliated Hospital of Sun Yat-sen University (Approval Number: 2023-001), and all participants provided written informed consent.

**Inclusion criteria for MDD group**:
- Age 8-80 years (adolescents under 18 years required guardian consent)
- Diagnosis of major depressive disorder according to DSM-5 criteria (confirmed by psychiatrists)
- No history of ocular disease or surgery
- Best-corrected visual acuity ≥ 1.0
- Clear optical media allowing good quality OCT imaging
- Voluntary participation with signed informed consent (for participants under 18 years, both guardian and participant consent were obtained)

**Note on depression severity assessment**: MDD diagnosis was established by psychiatrists based on DSM-5 criteria, independent of symptom severity at the time of enrollment. PHQ-9 was administered on the same day as OCT scanning to assess current symptom severity. Patients with PHQ-9 < 5 were considered to be in remission or receiving effective treatment, which is common in clinical practice where patients maintain their diagnosis while symptoms are controlled.

**Inclusion criteria for control group**:
- Age 8-80 years (adolescents under 18 years required guardian consent)
- No current or lifetime history of psychiatric disorders
- No family history of major psychiatric disorders in first-degree relatives
- Same ocular inclusion criteria as MDD group
- Voluntary participation with signed informed consent (for participants under 18 years, both guardian and participant consent were obtained)

**Exclusion criteria for both groups**:
- History of ocular trauma or surgery
- Glaucoma or ocular hypertension
- Diabetic retinopathy or other retinal diseases (e.g., uveitis, pathological myopia, macular diseases, optic neuropathy)
- Amblyopia or severe refractive media opacity
- Neurological disorders affecting the visual system
- Systemic diseases affecting the retina (e.g., diabetes mellitus, hypertension, optic neuritis, multiple sclerosis)
- Current use of medications known to affect retinal structure (e.g., chloroquine, hydroxychloroquine)
- Other psychiatric disorders or central nervous system diseases (e.g., cerebrovascular disease, Alzheimer's disease, multiple system atrophy)
- Substance use disorders within the past 6 months
- Pregnancy or lactation

## 2.2 Clinical Assessment

All participants underwent comprehensive clinical evaluation. Demographic information including age, sex, and education level was collected. Depression severity was assessed using the Patient Health Questionnaire-9 (PHQ-9), a validated 9-item self-report scale (Kroenke et al., 2001). Anxiety symptoms were measured using the Generalized Anxiety Disorder-7 (GAD-7) scale (Spitzer et al., 2006). All assessments were conducted by trained research staff blinded to OCT results.

**PHQ-9 data availability**: PHQ-9 data were available for 132 of 164 MDD patients (80.5%). Data were missing for 32 patients because PHQ-9 assessment was added to the study protocol after enrollment had already begun for the initial cohort. We compared baseline characteristics between patients with and without PHQ-9 data and found no significant differences in age (P=0.72), sex distribution (P=0.58), or any OCT parameter (all P>0.30), suggesting data were missing at random (Supplementary Table S1).

## 2.3 OCT Imaging

OCT imaging was performed using the 3D OCT-2000 (Topcon Corporation, Tokyo, Japan) by experienced technicians blinded to participants' clinical status. All scans were obtained after pupillary dilation with 1% tropicamide.

**Macular imaging**: A 6×6 mm macular cube scan centered on the fovea was acquired. The ETDRS grid was used to divide the macula into nine regions (Early Treatment Diabetic Retinopathy Study Research Group, 1991): central, inner superior, inner nasal, inner inferior, inner temporal, outer superior, outer nasal, outer inferior, and outer temporal (**Supplementary Figure S2A**). Automated segmentation was used to obtain thickness measurements for five retinal layers (**Supplementary Figure S2B**): retinal nerve fiber layer (RNFL), ganglion cell-inner plexiform layer (GC-IPL), ganglion cell complex (GCC), outer retina, and choroid.

**Optic disc imaging**: A 3D disc scan was performed to obtain peripapillary RNFL thickness measurements in four quadrants (**Supplementary Figure S2C**: superior, nasal, inferior, temporal) and overall average. Optic disc parameters including disc area, cup area, rim area, cup-to-disc ratio (vertical and horizontal), and rim volume were automatically calculated.

**Quality control**: Only scans with signal strength ≥ 6 and without motion artifacts were included. Poor quality scans were repeated. If acceptable quality could not be achieved after three attempts, the participant was excluded from the analysis.

## 2.4 Statistical Analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using Python 3.12 with SciPy 1.17.1, statsmodels 0.14.6, and scikit-learn 1.8.0. A two-tailed P-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

**Descriptive statistics**: Continuous variables were expressed as mean ± standard deviation or median (interquartile range) depending on distribution. Categorical variables were expressed as frequencies and percentages.

**Group comparisons**: Differences between MDD patients and controls were assessed using Mann-Whitney U tests for continuous variables and chi-square tests for categorical variables. For multiple comparisons of OCT parameters, false discovery rate (FDR) correction was applied using the Benjamini-Hochberg method (Benjamini and Hochberg, 1995), with q-values reported alongside raw P-values. Effect sizes were calculated using Cohen's d with 95% confidence intervals (CI) (Cohen, 1988). Mean differences between groups were also reported with 95% CI using Welch's t-test approximation.

**Multiple comparison correction**: Given the large number of OCT parameters analyzed (n=73), we controlled for false discovery rate (FDR) using the Benjamini-Hochberg procedure. All P values across the 73 parameters were ranked and adjusted simultaneously to produce q values. Statistical significance was defined as q<0.05. For transparency, we report both uncorrected P values and FDR-adjusted q values in all tables and supplementary materials.

We classified results into three categories:
- **Definitive evidence:** q<0.05 (FDR-controlled significant)
- **Suggestive evidence:** P<0.05 but q>0.05 (nominal significance only)
- **Non-significant:** P≥0.05

Results in the "suggestive" category are reported for completeness but interpreted with appropriate caution regarding false positive risk.

**Correlation analysis**: Associations between depression severity (PHQ-9 scores) and OCT parameters were assessed using Spearman's rank correlation coefficients.

**Diagnostic performance**: Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves were constructed to evaluate diagnostic performance. The area under the curve (AUC) with 95% confidence intervals was calculated using the bootstrap method (2000 iterations). The optimal cutoff point for each parameter was determined using the Youden index (J = Sensitivity + Specificity - 1).

**Multivariate analysis**: Multiple linear regression was used to assess the independent association between depression status and OCT parameters after controlling for age, sex, and education level. Regression coefficients (β) with 95% CI, standard errors, and model diagnostics (R², adjusted R², F-statistic) were reported. Model assumptions including residual normality (Shapiro-Wilk test), homoscedasticity (Breusch-Pagan test), and multicollinearity (variance inflation factor) were checked.

**Age adjustment**: Age was included as a continuous covariate in all multivariable models. We first examined the distribution of age in both groups and confirmed that the assumption of linearity was reasonable. To test for potential non-linear associations, we fitted additional models including a quadratic age term (age²) and performed likelihood ratio tests comparing linear vs. quadratic models. No significant improvement in model fit was observed (all P>0.10), supporting the use of linear age adjustment. We also conducted sensitivity analyses stratifying participants by age tertiles (<35, 35-50, >50 years) and found consistent effect estimates across strata (P for interaction >0.30), further confirming the appropriateness of linear adjustment.

**Mixed-effects models**: To account for the correlation between two eyes from the same participant, we fitted linear mixed-effects models (LMMs) with participant ID as a random intercept. The model structure was:

**Y_ij = β₀ + β₁Group + β₂Age + β₃Sex + u_i + ε_ij**

Where:
- Y_ij = OCT parameter for eye j of participant i
- β₀ = overall intercept
- β₁, β₂, β₃ = fixed effects coefficients
- u_i ~ N(0, σ²_u) = random intercept for participant i (between-participant variation)
- ε_ij ~ N(0, σ²_ε) = residual error (within-participant variation)

The intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC) was calculated as ICC = σ²_u / (σ²_u + σ²_ε) to quantify the proportion of variance attributable to between-participant differences.

Model fit was assessed using Akaike Information Criterion (AIC) and Bayesian Information Criterion (BIC). As a sensitivity analysis, we compared LMM results with participant-level analyses using the average of both eyes per participant. Effect estimates were consistent between approaches (difference in β coefficients <10%), supporting the robustness of our findings regardless of analytical approach.

**Subgroup analysis**: MDD patients were stratified by depression severity (mild: PHQ-9 5-9, moderate: 10-14, severe: ≥15) to explore potential dose-response relationships. Trends across severity groups were assessed using the Jonckheere-Terpstra test.

**Inter-eye consistency**: Pearson correlation coefficients were calculated between right and left eye measurements to assess inter-eye consistency.

**Sample size**: Based on previous studies showing approximately 5-10 μm difference in macular thickness between depressed patients and controls, with standard deviation of 15-20 μm, a sample size of 120 per group would provide 80% power to detect significant differences at α=0.05. Primary analyses were conducted at the eye level (n=499 eyes) to maximize statistical power. To account for inter-eye correlation within participants, we used linear mixed-effects models with participant ID as a random intercept (Pinheiro and Bates, 2000). Sensitivity analyses using participant-level averages yielded consistent results.
