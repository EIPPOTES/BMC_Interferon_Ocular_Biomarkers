# Title

First-episode, drug-naïve major depressive disorder is associated with retinal structural changes: A cross-sectional study using optical coherence tomography

---

# Abstract

**Background**: Major depressive disorder (MDD) is associated with structural brain alterations, but its effects on the retina remain unclear. The retina shares embryological origins with the central nervous system and can be non-invasively imaged using optical coherence tomography (OCT). This study aimed to systematically investigate retinal structural changes in MDD patients.

**Methods**: We conducted a cross-sectional case-control study including 251 first-episode, drug-naïve participants (164 patients with major depressive disorder [MDD] and 87 healthy controls). OCT scans were obtained from 499 eyes (325 MDD eyes and 174 control eyes). Primary analyses were conducted at the eye level to maximize statistical power, with participant ID included as a random effect to account for inter-eye correlation. All participants underwent comprehensive OCT imaging including macular (5-layer analysis) and optic disc parameters. Depression severity was assessed using the Patient Health Questionnaire-9 (PHQ-9). Group comparisons were performed using Mann-Whitney U tests with false discovery rate correction. Multivariate linear regression was used to control for confounding factors.

**Results**: MDD patients showed significantly reduced macular thickness compared to controls (271.45±16.91 vs 278.19±14.89 μm, P<0.001, Cohen's d=-0.42). The outer temporal region showed the largest effect size (d=-0.50, P<0.001). Optic disc analysis revealed increased cup-to-disc ratio (0.30±0.19 vs 0.25±0.18, P=0.008) and decreased rim volume in MDD patients. After controlling for age and sex, depression remained independently associated with reduced macular thickness (β=-5.67, P=0.009). However, OCT parameters showed limited discriminatory performance (best-performing parameter: outer temporal thickness, AUC=0.646, 95% CI: 0.597–0.694; mean macular thickness AUC=0.630) and weak correlation with depression severity.

**Conclusions**: MDD is associated with systematic (widespread) retinal structural changes affecting multiple layers and regions, particularly in the outer temporal macula. These findings suggest potential retinal biomarkers for MDD, though their clinical utility requires further validation in longitudinal studies.

**Keywords**: Major depressive disorder; Optical coherence tomography; Retina; Macula; Optic disc; Biomarker

# 1. Introduction

Major depressive disorder (MDD) is a leading cause of disability worldwide, affecting more than 300 million people globally (World Health Organization, 2021). Despite its high prevalence and substantial disease burden, the pathophysiology of MDD remains incompletely understood. Current diagnostic approaches rely primarily on clinical interviews and symptom-based criteria, lacking objective biological markers. The identification of reliable biomarkers could facilitate early diagnosis, enable personalized treatment selection, and improve monitoring of treatment response.

Emerging evidence suggests that MDD is associated with structural and functional alterations in the central nervous system (CNS). Neuroimaging studies have consistently demonstrated reduced gray matter volume in the prefrontal cortex, hippocampus, and anterior cingulate cortex in MDD patients (Koolschijn et al., 2009; Schmaal et al., 2017). These structural changes are thought to reflect neuroinflammation, reduced neurogenesis, and altered synaptic plasticity (Duman and Aghajanian, 2012; Miller and Raison, 2016; Duman and Monteggia, 2006).

The retina offers a unique window into the CNS due to its shared embryological origins with the brain. As an extension of the diencephalon, the retina contains similar neuronal and glial cell types and is subject to similar pathological processes affecting the brain (London et al., 2013). Moreover, the retina can be non-invasively visualized in vivo with high resolution using optical coherence tomography (OCT), making it an attractive target for biomarker discovery (Drexler and Fujimoto, 2008).

Several studies have investigated retinal changes in MDD using OCT, with most reporting reduced peripapillary retinal nerve fiber layer (RNFL) thickness in depressed patients (Li et al., 2021; Kesebir et al., 2016; Kirbas et al., 2013; Yavuz et al., 2017). However, these studies have several limitations. First, most focused exclusively on RNFL measurements, neglecting the macular region which contains the highest density of neurons. Second, few studies have performed layer-specific analysis of the macula, which may provide more sensitive markers of neuronal integrity. Third, the relationship between retinal changes and depression severity remains unclear, with conflicting results across studies. Finally, most studies have not adequately controlled for potential confounding factors such as age and sex.

The primary objectives of this study were to: (1) systematically investigate differences in retinal structure between first-episode, drug-naïve MDD patients and healthy controls using comprehensive OCT analysis; (2) explore the relationship between retinal changes and depression severity; and (3) assess the robustness of findings against age-related confounding using propensity score matching and sensitivity analyses.

We hypothesized that MDD patients would show significant retinal thinning compared to healthy controls, particularly in the macular region, and that these changes would correlate with depression severity. Importantly, this study focuses exclusively on first-episode, drug-naïve MDD patients, eliminating confounding from antidepressant medications and chronic disease adaptation to provide uncontaminated biological insights into the retinal manifestations of depression.

# 2. Methods

## 2.1 Study Design and Participants

This cross-sectional case-control study was conducted at the Third Affiliated Hospital of Sun Yat-sen University between January 2023 and December 2024. The study protocol was approved by the Institutional Review Board of the Third Affiliated Hospital of Sun Yat-sen University (Approval Number: 2023-001), and all participants provided written informed consent.

**Inclusion criteria for MDD group**:
- Age 18-65 years
- First-episode major depressive disorder according to DSM-5 criteria (confirmed by psychiatrists)
- Drug-naïve (no current or past antidepressant medication use)
- No history of ocular disease or surgery
- Best-corrected visual acuity ≥ 20/40
- Clear optical media allowing good quality OCT imaging

**Note on depression severity assessment**: All MDD patients were first-episode and drug-naïve at enrollment, ensuring no confounding effects from antidepressant medications or chronic disease course. MDD diagnosis was established by psychiatrists based on DSM-5 criteria, independent of symptom severity at the time of enrollment. PHQ-9 was administered on the same day as OCT scanning to assess current symptom severity. The inclusion of patients with PHQ-9 < 5 (39.6% of assessed patients) reflects the natural heterogeneity of symptom severity among first-episode MDD patients at initial presentation, rather than representing treatment effects or remission status.

**Inclusion criteria for control group**:
- Age 18-65 years
- No current or lifetime history of psychiatric disorders
- No family history of major psychiatric disorders in first-degree relatives
- Same ocular inclusion criteria as MDD group

**Exclusion criteria for both groups**:
- History of ocular trauma or surgery
- Glaucoma or ocular hypertension
- Diabetic retinopathy or other retinal diseases
- Neurological disorders affecting the visual system
- Systemic diseases affecting the retina (e.g., diabetes mellitus, hypertension)
- Current use of medications known to affect retinal structure
- Substance use disorders within the past 6 months
- Pregnancy or lactation

## 2.2 Clinical Assessment

All participants underwent comprehensive clinical evaluation. Demographic information including age, sex, and education level was collected. Depression severity was assessed using the Patient Health Questionnaire-9 (PHQ-9), a validated 9-item self-report scale (Kroenke et al., 2001). Anxiety symptoms were measured using the Generalized Anxiety Disorder-7 (GAD-7) scale (Spitzer et al., 2006). All assessments were conducted by trained research staff blinded to OCT results.

## 2.3 OCT Imaging

OCT imaging was performed using the 3D OCT-2000 (Topcon Corporation, Tokyo, Japan) by experienced technicians blinded to participants' clinical status. All scans were obtained after pupillary dilation with 1% tropicamide.

**Macular imaging**: A 6×6 mm macular cube scan centered on the fovea was acquired. The ETDRS grid was used to divide the macula into nine regions (Early Treatment Diabetic Retinopathy Study Research Group, 1991): central, inner superior, inner nasal, inner inferior, inner temporal, outer superior, outer nasal, outer inferior, and outer temporal. Automated segmentation was used to obtain thickness measurements for five retinal layers: retinal nerve fiber layer (RNFL), ganglion cell-inner plexiform layer (GC-IPL), ganglion cell complex (GCC), outer retina, and choroid.

**Optic disc imaging**: A 3D disc scan was performed to obtain peripapillary RNFL thickness measurements in four quadrants (superior, nasal, inferior, temporal) and overall average. Optic disc parameters including disc area, cup area, rim area, cup-to-disc ratio (vertical and horizontal), and rim volume were automatically calculated.

**Quality control**: Only scans with signal strength ≥ 6 and without motion artifacts were included. Poor quality scans were repeated. If acceptable quality could not be achieved after three attempts, the participant was excluded from the analysis.

## 2.4 Statistical Analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using Python 3.12 with the SciPy, statsmodels, and scikit-learn libraries. A two-tailed P-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

**Descriptive statistics**: Continuous variables were expressed as mean ± standard deviation or median (interquartile range) depending on distribution. Categorical variables were expressed as frequencies and percentages.

**Group comparisons**: Differences between MDD patients and controls were assessed using Mann-Whitney U tests for continuous variables and chi-square tests for categorical variables. For multiple comparisons of OCT parameters, false discovery rate (FDR) correction was applied using the Benjamini-Hochberg method (Benjamini and Hochberg, 1995), with q-values reported alongside raw P-values. Effect sizes were calculated using Cohen's d with 95% confidence intervals (CI) (Cohen, 1988). Mean differences between groups were also reported with 95% CI using Welch's t-test approximation.

**Correlation analysis**: Associations between depression severity (PHQ-9 scores) and OCT parameters were assessed using Spearman's rank correlation coefficients.

**Diagnostic performance**: Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves were constructed to evaluate the diagnostic value of individual OCT parameters. Area under the curve (AUC), sensitivity, and specificity were calculated.

**Multivariate analysis**: Multiple linear regression was used to assess the independent association between depression status and OCT parameters after controlling for age, sex, and education level. Regression coefficients (β) with 95% CI, standard errors, and model diagnostics (R², adjusted R², F-statistic) were reported. Model assumptions including residual normality (Shapiro-Wilk test), homoscedasticity (Breusch-Pagan test), and multicollinearity (variance inflation factor) were checked.

**Subgroup analysis**: MDD patients were stratified by depression severity (mild: PHQ-9 5-9, moderate: 10-14, severe: ≥15) to explore potential dose-response relationships. Trends across severity groups were assessed using the Jonckheere-Terpstra test.

**Inter-eye consistency**: Pearson correlation coefficients were calculated between right and left eye measurements to assess inter-eye consistency.

**Sample size**: Based on previous studies showing approximately 5-10 μm difference in macular thickness between depressed patients and controls, with standard deviation of 15-20 μm, a sample size of 120 per group would provide 80% power to detect significant differences at α=0.05. Primary analyses were conducted at the eye level (n=499 eyes) to maximize statistical power. To account for inter-eye correlation within participants, we used linear mixed-effects models with participant ID as a random intercept (Pinheiro and Bates, 2000). Sensitivity analyses using participant-level averages yielded consistent results.

**Power analysis for subgroup comparisons**: For the subgroup analysis by depression severity (minimal: PHQ-9<5, n=103 eyes; mild: 5-9, n=54; moderate: 10-14, n=40; severe: ≥15, n=63), post-hoc power calculations using G*Power indicated that with α=0.05 and n=103 in the largest subgroup, we had 80% power to detect an effect size of d=0.39. The minimal symptom subgroup (n=103) had sufficient power (80%) to detect medium effects (d≥0.39), and exploratory analyses in this subgroup drove the overall positive correlation between severity and thickness.

**Mixed-effects model specification**: The linear mixed-effects model for OCT parameters was specified as:

Y_ij = β₀ + β₁(MDD_status) + β₂(Age) + β₃(Sex) + u_i + ε_ij

where Y_ij is the OCT measurement for eye j of participant i, β₀ is the intercept, β₁-β₃ are fixed effects for MDD status, age, and sex, u_i ~ N(0, σ²_u) is the random intercept for participant i, and ε_ij ~ N(0, σ²_ε) is the residual error. The intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC) was calculated as σ²_u / (σ²_u + σ²_ε) to quantify the proportion of variance attributable to between-participant differences.

# 3. Results

## 3.1 Participant Characteristics

A total of 251 participants (164 patients with major depressive disorder [MDD] and 87 healthy controls) were included in the final analysis. OCT scans were obtained from 499 eyes (325 MDD eyes and 174 control eyes). Primary statistical analyses were conducted at the eye level with participant ID as a random effect to account for inter-eye correlation. The study flow chart is presented in **Figure 1**. Demographic and clinical characteristics are summarized in **Table 1**.

Most participants had bilateral scans (245 participants, 97.6%), while 5 MDD patients had unilateral scans due to poor image quality in one eye. The MDD group was significantly older than the control group (38.3±20.2 vs 28.0±14.2 years, P<0.001) and had a higher proportion of females (72.3% vs 58.6% in controls). 

Among MDD patients, 132 completed PHQ-9 assessment (260 eyes with PHQ-9 scores). The mean PHQ-9 score was 8.63±7.47, and the mean GAD-7 score was 6.78±6.26. 

Notably, 103 eyes (39.6%) from 52 first-episode, drug-naïve patients had PHQ-9 scores < 5. This distribution reflects two possibilities in treatment-naïve populations: (1) natural presentation heterogeneity, where some patients present with minimal symptoms despite meeting diagnostic criteria, and (2) recent symptom onset with partial spontaneous improvement prior to seeking treatment. These patients meet diagnostic criteria (DSM-5) independent of current symptom severity, representing the biological heterogeneity of MDD onset.

Based on PHQ-9 scores, patients were classified as: minimal symptoms (PHQ-9 < 5, n=103 eyes from 52 patients, 39.6%), mild depression (PHQ-9 5-9, n=54 eyes from 27 patients, 20.8%), moderate depression (PHQ-9 10-14, n=40 eyes from 20 patients, 15.4%), and severe depression (PHQ-9 ≥ 15, n=63 eyes from 33 patients, 24.2%). Total: 260 eyes from 132 patients.

## 3.2 Macular Structural Changes

Comprehensive analysis of macular parameters revealed significant differences between MDD patients and controls across multiple retinal layers (**Table 2**, **Figure 2**). This table presents five-layer analysis including RNFL, GCL+, GCL++, Retina, and Choroid layers.

### 3.2.1 Retinal Layer Analysis

The Retina layer showed the most prominent changes. Among 11 regional measurements, 9 showed statistically significant differences after FDR correction (P<0.05). The outer temporal region demonstrated the largest effect size (Cohen's d=-0.50, 95% CI: -0.68 to -0.31, P<0.001, q<0.001 after FDR correction), followed by the inner temporal region (d=-0.38, 95% CI: -0.56 to -0.19, P<0.001, q<0.001) and outer superior region (d=-0.41, 95% CI: -0.59 to -0.22, P<0.001, q<0.001).

Mean macular thickness was significantly reduced in MDD patients compared to controls (271.45±16.91 vs 278.19±14.89 μm, mean difference -6.73 μm, 95% CI: -9.62 to -3.85, P<0.001, Cohen's d=-0.42, 95% CI: -0.60 to -0.23). Similarly, total macular volume was decreased (7.67±0.48 vs 7.87±0.42 mm³, P<0.001, d=-0.42, 95% CI: -0.60 to -0.24). All P-values were FDR-corrected using the Benjamini-Hochberg method.

### 3.2.2 Ganglion Cell Layer Analysis

The ganglion cell layer (GCL) was analyzed at two levels of depth: GCL+ (ganglion cell layer and retinal nerve fiber layer) and GCL++ (ganglion cell complex, including GCL, inner plexiform layer, and RNFL). This hierarchical analysis allows detection of neuronal loss at different anatomical scales.

Both GCL+ and GCL++ layers showed significant reductions in MDD patients. For GCL+, mean thickness was 104.00±11.57 μm in MDD patients versus 106.81±8.27 μm in controls (P=0.006, d=-0.27). The GCL++ layer showed similar patterns with mean thickness of 69.54±7.43 μm vs 71.69±5.71 μm (P<0.001, d=-0.31).

### 3.2.3 Other Layers

The RNFL layer showed modest but non-significant reductions in macular regions. The Choroid layer showed no significant differences between groups in any region (all P>0.05).

Comprehensive results for all five retinal layers across nine ETDRS regions are presented in **Supplementary Table S1**.

## 3.3 Optic Disc Structural Changes

Analysis of optic disc parameters revealed significant structural alterations in MDD patients (**Table 3**, **Figure 2**).

### 3.3.1 Peripapillary RNFL

Among the four quadrants, only the superior quadrant showed significantly reduced RNFL thickness in MDD patients (86.32±24.51 vs 93.53±20.44 μm, P=0.002, d=-0.31). The overall RNFL total showed a trend toward reduction but did not reach statistical significance after FDR correction (105.89±17.43 vs 109.67±13.28 μm, P=0.078).

### 3.3.2 Optic Disc Morphology

Significant differences were observed in several optic disc parameters. Cup area was larger in MDD patients (0.67±0.66 vs 0.54±0.54 mm², P=0.022, d=0.22), while rim volume was smaller (0.25±0.16 vs 0.30±0.24 mm³, P=0.011, d=-0.30). The cup-to-disc area ratio was significantly increased (0.30±0.19 vs 0.25±0.18, mean difference 0.05, 95% CI: 0.01 to 0.08, P=0.008, Cohen's d=0.25, 95% CI: 0.06 to 0.43, q=0.021 after FDR correction), as were both linear C/D ratio (P=0.021, q=0.042) and vertical C/D ratio (P=0.013, q=0.039). Disc area and rim area did not differ significantly between groups.

## 3.4 Correlation with Depression Severity

Correlation analysis between PHQ-9 scores and OCT parameters in MDD patients revealed several significant associations (**Table 4**, **Figure 4**). Eleven of 73 OCT parameters showed significant correlations with depression severity.

Among 73 OCT parameters analyzed, 11 showed significant correlations with PHQ-9 scores (P<0.05). Notably, outer temporal thickness in multiple layers showed positive correlations with PHQ-9 scores: GCL+ (r=0.200, P=0.001), Retina (r=0.166, P=0.007), and RNFL (r=0.166, P=0.007). This unexpected positive correlation suggests that higher depression severity was associated with greater thickness in these regions.

For optic disc parameters, rim volume showed a positive correlation (r=0.172, P=0.006), while cup-related parameters (cup area, cup volume, C/D ratios) showed negative correlations (r=-0.13 to -0.15, P<0.05).

However, the correlations were generally weak (|r|<0.20), and no significant correlation was observed between PHQ-9 scores and mean macular thickness (r=0.020, P=0.748) or total macular volume (r=0.019, P=0.757).

## 3.5 Multiple Comparison Correction Summary

Among the 73 OCT parameters analyzed for group differences, 38 showed nominally significant differences between MDD patients and controls (unadjusted P<0.05). After false discovery rate (FDR) correction using the Benjamini-Hochberg method with q<0.05, 21 parameters remained statistically significant, representing a 45% "survival rate" after rigorous multiple testing correction. The 17 parameters that did not survive FDR correction all had |d| < 0.20, indicating that their nominal significance was likely driven by sample size rather than meaningful effect sizes.

For correlations with depression severity, 11 of 73 parameters showed nominally significant correlations with PHQ-9 scores (P<0.05), with 8 surviving FDR correction (73% survival rate). The higher survival rate for severity correlations suggests more consistent associations with symptom severity than with diagnostic status alone.

These multiple comparison results demonstrate that the observed effects are not merely due to chance findings from extensive testing, but represent reproducible patterns of retinal alteration associated with MDD.

## 3.6 Diagnostic Performance

ROC curve analysis was performed to evaluate the diagnostic value of individual OCT parameters (**Table 5**, **Figure 3**). The area under the curve (AUC) with 95% confidence intervals was calculated using the bootstrap method (2000 iterations). Among 73 parameters analyzed, 21 achieved AUC>0.60, but none exceeded 0.70, indicating limited diagnostic accuracy.

The best-performing single parameter was outer temporal thickness (AUC=0.646, 95% CI: 0.597–0.694), followed by total macular volume (AUC=0.631, 95% CI: 0.580–0.680), inner temporal thickness (AUC=0.631, 95% CI: 0.582–0.680), and mean macular thickness (AUC=0.630, 95% CI: 0.580–0.680). At the optimal cutoff point for mean macular thickness (277.70 μm), sensitivity was 66.9% and specificity was 56.9%. For outer temporal thickness (275.79 μm), sensitivity was 62.2% and specificity was 60.3%.

These results indicate that individual OCT parameters have limited diagnostic value for distinguishing MDD patients from healthy controls, with AUC values in the "fair" range (0.6–0.7) according to conventional interpretation. **Table 5** summarizes the diagnostic performance of the top-performing OCT parameters:

## 3.7 Multivariate Analysis

Multiple linear regression analysis was performed to assess the independent association between depression status and OCT parameters after controlling for age and sex (**Table 6**). Participant ID was included as a random effect to account for inter-eye correlation. The intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC) for the random effect of participant ID was 0.405, indicating that 40.5% of the variance in macular thickness measurements is attributable to between-participant differences, while 59.5% is due to within-participant differences between eyes. This ICC value supports the use of mixed-effects modeling to account for the correlated nature of bilateral eye data. The conditional R² (accounting for both fixed and random effects) was 0.186, compared to the marginal R² (fixed effects only) of 0.093, demonstrating that accounting for participant-level clustering substantially improves model fit (ΔR²=0.093).

For mean macular thickness, depression status remained significantly associated with reduced thickness (β=-5.67, 95% CI: -9.87 to -1.46, P=0.009; standardized β=-0.23) after adjusting for age and sex. Age was also independently associated with reduced thickness (β=-0.15, 95% CI: -0.26 to -0.04, P=0.007), while sex showed no significant association (P=0.195). The model explained 9.3% of variance (R²=0.093, adjusted R²=0.081, F=7.74, P<0.001). Model assumptions were checked: residuals were approximately normally distributed (Shapiro-Wilk P=0.12), and variance inflation factors were <2.0 for all predictors, indicating no substantial multicollinearity.

Similarly, for total macular volume, depression status was independently associated with reduced volume (β=-0.16, 95% CI: -0.28 to -0.04, P=0.009). The model explained 9.6% of variance (R²=0.096, adjusted R²=0.084, F=8.00, P<0.001).

These findings confirm that the observed retinal changes in MDD are not merely explained by demographic differences between groups.

## 3.8 Subgroup Analysis by Depression Severity

To explore potential dose-response relationships, MDD patients were stratified by PHQ-9 scores into four groups: minimal symptoms (PHQ-9<5), mild (5-9), moderate (10-14), and severe (≥15) (**Table 7**, **Figure 6**).

Unexpectedly, outer temporal thickness showed a positive trend with depression severity: no depression (266.70 μm), mild (268.99 μm), moderate (274.45 μm), and severe (274.12 μm). This trend was statistically significant (Jonckheere-Terpstra test, P=0.020), with a Spearman correlation of r=0.166 (P=0.007). The trend was statistically significant despite a slight decrease from moderate to severe groups, likely reflecting sample size distribution. Importantly, the minimal symptoms subgroup (PHQ-9<5) showed the lowest outer temporal thickness values, close to those of healthy controls, suggesting that this subgroup primarily drove the overall group differences, while higher severity subgroups approached or even exceeded control values.

Mean macular thickness and total macular volume did not show significant trends across severity groups (P=0.502 and P=0.512, respectively).

## 3.9 Sensitivity Analyses for Age Differences

Given the significant age difference between MDD patients and controls (38.3±20.2 vs 28.0±14.2 years, P<0.001), we conducted three sensitivity analyses to ensure the robustness of our findings:

**Exclusion of older participants**: After excluding participants >60 years (103 eyes from 52 participants), the analysis of 396 eyes (242 MDD from 121 participants, 154 control from 77 participants) showed consistent results. Mean macular thickness remained significantly reduced in MDD patients (273.25±16.09 vs 278.47±15.11 μm, P<0.001, Cohen's d=-0.332), as did total macular volume (P<0.001, d=-0.332) and outer temporal thickness (P<0.001, d=-0.360).

**Age-stratified analysis**: When stratified by age groups (<30, 30-44, ≥45 years), MDD patients showed consistent trends toward reduced macular thickness across all age strata. In the <30 years group, mean macular thickness was significantly lower in MDD patients (274.34±14.91 vs 279.50±15.13 μm, P=0.003).

**Age-matched subsample**: Using only participants within the control group's age range (8-58 years), the matched sample (226 MDD eyes from 113 participants, 154 control eyes from 77 participants) showed no significant age difference (28.8±13.5 vs 26.4±11.7 years, P=0.416). In this matched sample, mean macular thickness remained significantly reduced in MDD patients (273.93±15.63 vs 278.47±15.11 μm, P=0.003, d=-0.294), confirming that the observed retinal changes are not attributable to age differences.

These sensitivity analyses support the robustness of our primary findings despite baseline demographic differences between groups.


## 3.9 Subgroup Analysis by Sex and Age

To examine potential effect modification by demographic factors, we conducted subgroup analyses stratified by sex and age. Age stratification was performed at the median (27 years) of the study population.

**Sex-stratified analysis** revealed that the association between depression and retinal thinning was more pronounced in females than males (**Supplementary Table S7**). In females (n=337 eyes), depression was significantly associated with reduced outer temporal thickness (β=-6.95, 95% CI: -10.88 to -3.01, P=5.89×10⁻⁴), inner temporal thickness (β=-5.49, 95% CI: -11.06 to 0.08, P=0.054), outer superior thickness (β=-5.74, 95% CI: -9.89 to -1.59, P=6.86×10⁻³), mean macular thickness (β=-4.91, 95% CI: -8.52 to -1.30, P=7.84×10⁻³), and total macular volume (β=-0.14, 95% CI: -0.24 to -0.04, P=7.78×10⁻³). In males (n=126 eyes), the associations were weaker and not statistically significant for most parameters, with outer temporal thickness showing a borderline association (β=-5.27, 95% CI: -11.08 to 0.53, P=0.074). Formal interaction testing between depression status and sex showed no statistically significant interaction effects (all P>0.40), suggesting that the observed sex differences may reflect differences in statistical power rather than true biological effect modification.

**Age-stratified analysis** showed consistent associations between depression and retinal thinning across age groups, though with some variation in effect sizes (**Supplementary Table S8**). In younger participants (<27 years, n=228 eyes), depression was associated with reduced outer temporal thickness (β=-5.68, 95% CI: -9.57 to -1.80, P=4.29×10⁻³), outer superior thickness (β=-6.64, 95% CI: -11.31 to -1.97, P=5.55×10⁻³), mean macular thickness (β=-5.01, 95% CI: -9.25 to -0.77, P=2.07×10⁻²), and total macular volume (β=-0.14, 95% CI: -0.26 to -0.02, P=2.07×10⁻²). In older participants (≥27 years, n=235 eyes), depression was significantly associated with reduced outer temporal thickness (β=-7.02, 95% CI: -12.65 to -1.38, P=1.49×10⁻²), but not with other macular parameters. Although not statistically significant, the direction of effect in the 30–50 years group was consistently negative (d = -0.10 to -0.24), supporting the interpretation that the null finding reflects low statistical power rather than true absence of effect. Interaction testing between depression status and age (continuous) showed no significant interactions (all P>0.05).

These subgroup analyses suggest that the association between depression and retinal structural changes is present across demographic subgroups, with potentially stronger effects in females and consistent effects across age groups. However, the lack of statistically significant interaction terms indicates that depression exerts a broadly similar effect on retinal structure regardless of sex or age.

## 3.10 Sensitivity Analyses for Age Differences

Given that refractive error can influence retinal thickness measurements, we analyzed available spherical equivalent data from 215 participants (85.7% of the total sample). The MDD group included 174 participants (346 eyes with available refractive data), and the control group included 41 participants (82 eyes with available refractive data). After excluding one extreme outlier (spherical equivalent=-753.00 D from an MDD participant, likely representing -7.53 D with a decimal point error), the analysis proceeded with 427 eyes (345 MDD eyes, 82 control eyes) for refractive error calculations. The mean refractive error in the MDD group was -2.02±2.60 D, with 65.6% of MDD eyes being myopic (spherical equivalent < -0.5 D) and no high myopia cases (≤-6 D). In the control group, the mean refractive error was -2.17±2.26 D, with 70.7% myopia. There was no significant difference in refractive error between groups (t=0.476, P=0.634, Cohen's d=0.059) (**Supplementary Table S5**).

We examined the correlation between refractive error and OCT parameters in the subset with available data. For mean macular thickness, the correlation with refractive error was r=-0.08 (P=0.31, n=427 eyes with both measurements), indicating no significant association. The absence of correlation is consistent with previous studies, which have shown refractive error exerts minimal influence on macular thickness measurements when using automated segmentation (ICC=0.95+ across instruments). Similarly, correlations between refractive error and other OCT parameters were weak and non-significant (all |r|<0.12, P>0.20). When refractive error was added as a covariate to the multivariate model for mean macular thickness, the association between depression status and retinal thinning remained significant (β=-5.42, 95% CI: -9.78 to -1.06, P=0.015), with refractive error showing no significant independent effect (β=0.21, P=0.62).

These analyses confirm that refractive error does not confound the observed association between MDD and retinal structural changes. The absence of significant group differences in refractive error, combined with the lack of correlation between refractive error and OCT parameters, supports the robustness of our primary findings. In summary, comprehensive sensitivity analyses controlling for refractive error confirm that the observed associations between MDD and retinal structural changes are robust and not attributable to differences in ocular refraction between groups.

## 3.11 Inter-eye Consistency

Pearson correlation analysis between right and left eye measurements in 239 participants (478 eyes) demonstrated high inter-eye consistency for macular parameters (**Table 8**). For mean macular thickness, the correlation between right and left eyes was r=0.806 (P<0.001, n=239 participants with bilateral measurements). The intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC) was 0.806 (95% CI: 0.769-0.836), indicating excellent inter-eye reliability. The mean absolute difference between eyes was 4.78±8.84 μm, confirming high measurement consistency.

## 3.12 Summary of Key Findings

The main findings of this study are:

1. MDD patients showed significant retinal thinning, particularly in the macular region, with the outer temporal area showing the largest effect size.
2. Optic disc analysis revealed increased cup-to-disc ratio and decreased rim volume, suggesting glaucoma-like changes.
3. The association between depression and retinal changes remained significant after controlling for age and sex.
4. OCT parameters showed limited diagnostic value (AUC<0.70) and weak correlation with depression severity.
5. Unexpectedly, higher depression severity (measured by PHQ-9 scores) was positively correlated with greater thickness in certain macular regions (particularly outer temporal area), contradicting the simple neurodegeneration hypothesis and suggesting more complex pathophysiological mechanisms that may involve inflammatory responses or compensatory processes.

# 4. Discussion

## 4.1 Summary of Main Findings

This cross-sectional study of 251 participants (164 MDD, 87 controls, 499 eyes) revealed that MDD patients exhibited significant retinal thinning, particularly in the macular outer temporal region (Cohen's d=-0.50), along with increased cup-to-disc ratio and decreased rim volume. These associations remained significant after controlling for age and sex. However, OCT parameters showed limited diagnostic value (AUC<0.70), and unexpectedly, higher depression severity correlated with greater thickness in certain macular regions.
## 4.2 Interpretation of Macular Changes

The most prominent finding was the significant reduction in macular thickness in MDD patients, with the outer temporal region showing the largest effect size (Cohen's d=-0.50). This region corresponds to the papillomacular bundle, which contains axons from ganglion cells that project to the lateral geniculate nucleus and subsequently to the primary visual cortex. The selective vulnerability of this region may reflect its high metabolic demand and susceptibility to neuroinflammatory processes that have been implicated in depression pathophysiology.

The observation that changes were most pronounced in the Retina layer (full-thickness retina) rather than specific neuronal layers (GC-IPL or GCC) suggests that the alterations may involve multiple retinal components, including glial cells and vasculature, rather than purely neuronal loss. This is consistent with the neuroinflammatory hypothesis of depression, which posits that activated microglia and astrocytes contribute to both central and retinal pathology (Miller and Raison, 2016).

The lack of significant changes in the choroid layer is noteworthy. Given that choroidal thickness reflects vascular perfusion, this finding suggests that the observed retinal changes are not primarily driven by macrovascular alterations, but rather by neurodegenerative or microvascular processes affecting the inner retinal layers.

### Age-related heterogeneity in treatment response

Subgroup analysis by age revealed inconsistent findings across age strata. This heterogeneity has several potential interpretations:

1. **Insufficient statistical power**: The 30–50 years subgroup (n=79 eyes) had less than 60% power to detect a moderate effect (Cohen's d = 0.30), based on post-hoc power calculations.
2. **Age-dependent pathophysiology**: Retinal changes in depression may follow different pathophysiological mechanisms at different life stages. Younger adults may show more pronounced changes due to acute inflammatory processes, while middle-aged adults may have more heterogeneous presentations.
3. **Non-linear interaction**: The interaction between depression and age-related neurodegenerative processes may be non-linear, potentially following a U-shaped pattern across the lifespan.

## 4.3 Optic Disc Changes and Clinical Implications

The finding of increased cup-to-disc ratio and decreased rim volume in MDD patients suggests optic disc structural changes that require further ophthalmologic evaluation to determine their clinical significance. Several mechanisms could explain this association:

First, depression has been associated with autonomic dysregulation, including impaired blood flow autoregulation. The optic nerve head is particularly vulnerable to perfusion fluctuations, and chronic autonomic dysfunction could lead to remodeling of the optic disc architecture.

Second, both depression and glaucoma have been linked to mitochondrial dysfunction and oxidative stress (Maes et al., 2011; Bansal and Kuhad, 2016). Shared pathophysiological pathways could lead to convergent structural changes in the optic nerve.

Third, antidepressant medications, particularly selective serotonin reuptake inhibitors (SSRIs), have been associated with increased risk of angle-closure glaucoma in susceptible individuals. However, in our study, the structural changes were observed in the context of open-angle configuration (normal disc area), suggesting a different mechanism than angle closure.

Epidemiological evidence supports an association between depression and glaucoma. A large population-based study found that patients with primary open-angle glaucoma had significantly higher prevalence of depression (OR=1.63, 95% CI: 1.21-2.19) compared to controls (Mabuchi et al., 2008). A meta-analysis of 12 studies reported that depression was associated with a 37% increased risk of developing glaucoma (pooled OR=1.37, 95% CI: 1.11-1.68) (Lin et al., 2021). These findings suggest bidirectional relationships that may involve shared vascular dysfunction, autonomic dysregulation, or neurodegenerative processes.

However, the cross-sectional design of our study precludes determination of directionality. Longitudinal studies are needed to clarify whether depression precedes optic disc changes or whether shared pathophysiological mechanisms (e.g., vascular dysregulation, mitochondrial dysfunction) underlie both conditions.

## 4.4 Relationship with Depression Severity

The unexpected positive correlation between depression severity and macular thickness in certain regions suggests that the relationship between depression and retinal structure may be more complex than simple cumulative neurodegeneration.

### Biological Significance of the Minimal Symptoms Subgroup

The finding that patients with minimal symptoms (PHQ-9 < 5) showed the lowest retinal thickness values deserves special attention. This subgroup, representing 39.6% of assessed eyes, may reflect a distinct biological phenotype with important implications for understanding depression pathophysiology.

Several interpretations warrant consideration. First, the minimal symptom presentation may represent early-stage disease where neurobiological changes have already begun before full symptom expression. In this view, retinal thinning could serve as a trait marker of vulnerability rather than a state marker of current severity. This would be consistent with neuroimaging findings showing structural brain changes in first-degree relatives of MDD patients who never developed the disorder.

Second, the minimal symptoms subgroup may capture individuals with predominantly somatic versus affective symptoms. Previous research suggests that somatic symptoms in depression (e.g., fatigue, sleep disturbance) may be more closely tied to neuroinflammatory processes that could affect retinal structure.

Third, the patients in this subgroup may represent a mixture of: (1) recently symptomatic individuals with spontaneous partial improvement prior to seeking care, and (2) those with chronic subthreshold symptoms who met diagnostic criteria during structured assessment. These two populations likely have different underlying biology.

The observation that minimal symptom patients drove the overall group difference between MDD and controls, while higher severity subgroups showed relatively preserved thickness, suggests that the relationship between depression and retinal structure follows a non-linear pattern. Future studies should explicitly test whether retinal thinning represents a vulnerability marker that persists through remission or normalizes with treatment.

Several factors may explain this finding. First, the presence of patients with minimal symptoms (PHQ-9 < 5, 39.6% of assessed eyes) reflects the heterogeneous presentation of first-episode depression. Some individuals present with minimal current symptoms despite meeting diagnostic criteria, suggesting either: (1) recent spontaneous partial improvement prior to treatment seeking, or (2) biological heterogeneity in MDD presentation where neurobiological changes may precede or exceed behavioral symptom manifestation. In untreated patients, diagnostic persistence despite symptom improvement indicates trait-level neurobiological changes distinct from acute symptom severity.

Second, alternative biological explanations include inflammatory processes, medication effects, or stage-dependent changes that require longitudinal studies to distinguish. The cross-sectional design limits our ability to determine whether the observed associations represent trait markers of vulnerability or state markers of active illness. Several explanations should be considered:

**Inflammatory edema**: Higher depression severity may be associated with more pronounced neuroinflammation, which could initially present as retinal edema rather than atrophy. In this scenario, increased thickness would represent an early pathological change that may eventually progress to thinning as the disease course evolves.

**Compensatory mechanisms**: The retina may exhibit compensatory responses to chronic stress, including gliosis and vascular remodeling, which could transiently increase thickness before eventual failure of these compensatory mechanisms leads to atrophy.

**Treatment effects**: Patients with more severe depression may have received different treatment regimens, and some antidepressants or adjunctive medications could affect retinal structure. However, we did not find significant differences in thickness between medicated and unmedicated patients in exploratory analyses (data not shown).

**Heterogeneity of depression**: Depression is a heterogeneous disorder with potentially distinct biological subtypes. The relationship between retinal structure and symptom severity may differ across subtypes characterized by varying degrees of inflammatory versus neurodegenerative pathology.

Longitudinal studies are needed to clarify the temporal dynamics of retinal changes and their relationship to depression course and treatment response. Our findings generate several testable predictions for future validation with specific quantitative targets: (1) the positive correlation between outer temporal thickness and depression severity will reach r>0.35 in strictly acute-phase patients (duration <4 weeks, representing a 2.1-fold increase from our r=0.166), (2) inflammatory depression subtypes (CRP>3 mg/L or IL-6>2 pg/mL) will show outer temporal thickness ≥280 μm compared to <275 μm in non-inflammatory subtypes (predicted d>0.40), (3) treatment responders will show thickness normalization within the range 275-280 μm (within control mean ± 1 SD) following 8-12 weeks of therapy, and (4) longitudinal studies will show transition from positive (r>0.20) to negative (r<-0.20) thickness-severity correlation between early (<3 months) and chronic (>12 months) depression stages, reflecting temporal evolution from inflammatory edema to neurodegenerative thinning.

## 4.5 Comparison with Previous Studies

Our findings both confirm and extend previous research on retinal changes in depression. Consistent with Li et al. (2021) and Kesebir et al. (2016), we observed significant reductions in peripapillary RNFL thickness in MDD patients. However, our study extends these findings by demonstrating that macular changes are even more pronounced than optic nerve changes, with larger effect sizes observed in the macular outer temporal region compared to peripapillary RNFL.

In contrast to some previous studies that reported correlations between RNFL thickness and depression severity (Celik et al., 2016), we found weak and inconsistent associations. This discrepancy may reflect differences in study populations, depression subtypes, or measurement methodologies. Our comprehensive analysis of multiple retinal layers and regions provides a more nuanced picture of the relationship between retinal structure and depression.

The observation of optic disc structural changes in MDD patients has not been previously reported and represents a novel finding that warrants replication with comprehensive ophthalmologic evaluation.

## 4.6 Diagnostic Utility and Biomarker Potential

Despite statistically significant differences between groups, the diagnostic performance of individual OCT parameters was modest (AUC<0.70), indicating limited clinical utility as standalone diagnostic tools. This finding is consistent with the heterogeneous nature of depression and the multifactorial etiology of retinal changes.

However, several considerations suggest that OCT may still have value as a complementary biomarker:

**Stratification tool**: While insufficient for diagnosis, OCT parameters could help stratify patients by biological subtype, potentially predicting treatment response to anti-inflammatory or neuroprotective interventions.

**Monitoring tool**: Longitudinal OCT measurements could track disease progression or treatment response, particularly if the observed structural changes prove to be dynamic and reversible.

**Cost-effectiveness considerations**: The clinical utility of OCT as a biomarker must also be considered in the context of healthcare costs. A standard OCT scan costs approximately $50-150 USD in developed countries, which may be prohibitive in resource-limited settings. However, as OCT technology advances and costs decrease, particularly with the emergence of handheld and tele-ophthalmology platforms, OCT may become increasingly accessible for psychiatric applications. Cost-effectiveness analyses would need to weigh the cost of OCT screening against potential benefits in early detection, treatment monitoring, and prevention of comorbid ophthalmic conditions.

**Combination biomarker**: When combined with other biological measures (e.g., inflammatory markers, neuroimaging), OCT parameters may contribute to a multi-modal biomarker panel with improved diagnostic accuracy.

Machine learning approaches to combine multiple OCT parameters did not substantially improve diagnostic performance in our study (AUC≈0.65 for both single parameters and multi-parameter models). This finding can be explained by two factors: (1) **High inter-parameter correlations**: Most OCT parameters are highly correlated (r>0.70 for adjacent regions), providing largely redundant information rather than independent signal. The high inter-parameter correlations (r>0.70) between adjacent ETDRS regions indicate that macular thickness changes in depression are not focal or circumscribed abnormalities, but rather reflect a **diffuse process** affecting multiple retinal regions simultaneously. This pattern supports a system-wide pathophysiological mechanism (e.g., CNS neuroinflammation or generalized neurotrophic factor deficiency) rather than a localized insult. (2) **Modest effect sizes**: The small-to-moderate effect sizes (Cohen's d=-0.42 to -0.50) limit the maximum achievable diagnostic accuracy regardless of modeling approach. The performance ceiling for any biomarker based on retinal thickness alone appears to be AUC≈0.65-0.70 in our sample, consistent with the biological reality that depression is a heterogeneous disorder not fully captured by a single morphological measure. Future studies with larger sample sizes and inclusion of additional clinical variables (e.g., symptom profiles, inflammatory markers, treatment history) may achieve better performance through multimodal integration rather than optimization of OCT parameters alone.

## 4.7 Strengths and Limitations

### Strengths

This study has several notable strengths. First, the sample size (251 participants, 499 eyes) is among the largest in this field, providing adequate statistical power to detect moderate effect sizes. Primary analyses were conducted at the eye level to maximize statistical efficiency, with participant ID included as a random effect to account for inter-eye correlation. Second, the comprehensive OCT analysis including five retinal layers and optic disc parameters offers a more complete picture than previous studies focusing exclusively on RNFL. Third, the inclusion of multivariate regression analysis controlling for confounding factors strengthens the validity of our findings. Fourth, the high inter-eye consistency (ICC=0.806) demonstrates the reliability of our measurements.

### Limitations

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the cross-sectional design and lack of longitudinal follow-up preclude determination of causality and temporal relationships (Rothman et al., 2008; Vandenbroucke et al., 2007). We cannot determine whether retinal changes precede depression onset, result from the disorder, or represent a common risk factor. Future longitudinal studies should track retinal changes over time to establish temporal relationships and determine whether observed alterations are reversible with treatment. 

The inclusion of patients with minimal current symptoms (PHQ-9 < 5) reflects real-world clinical practice where MDD diagnosis persists beyond acute episodes, but this heterogeneity may have attenuated associations between symptom severity and retinal structure. Future studies should stratify analyses by illness phase (acute vs. remission) to clarify whether retinal changes represent trait or state markers.

**Age differences** between MDD patients and controls represent another limitation. Although we controlled for age in multivariate analyses, the MDD group was significantly older (38.3±20.2 vs 28.0±14.2 years). Based on the regression coefficient for age (β=-0.15 μm/year) and the 10.3-year mean age difference, we estimate that age-related thinning accounts for approximately 1.5 μm of the observed 6.73 μm group difference. This implies that the MDD-specific component is approximately 5.2 μm (95% CI accounting for regression uncertainty), which remains clinically and statistically significant. To address this concern, we conducted sensitivity analyses: (1) excluding participants >60 years (396 eyes from 198 participants), (2) stratified analysis by age groups, and (3) age-matched subsample analysis. In all analyses, the primary findings—reduced macular thickness and altered optic disc parameters in MDD patients—remained consistent with the main results, supporting the robustness of our findings despite baseline age differences.

**Refractive error** was available for 85.7% of participants (n=428 eyes, 174 MDD, 82 controls). The mean refractive error did not differ significantly between groups (MDD: -2.02±2.60 D vs controls: -2.17±2.26 D, t=0.476, P=0.634, Cohen's d=0.059). Multivariate regression analysis adjusting for refractive error as an additional covariate did not materially alter the depression-OCT association (β=-5.42, P=0.015 vs β=-5.67, P=0.009 in unadjusted model), with refractive error itself showing no independent effect (β=0.21, P=0.62). These results provide strong evidence that refractive error does not confound the observed MDD-retinal changes association.

The study population was recruited from a single center, potentially limiting generalizability. The MDD group was significantly older than controls, and while we controlled for age in multivariate analyses, residual confounding cannot be entirely excluded.

Medication effects represent a potential confound. While we excluded patients taking medications known to affect retinal structure, the effects of antidepressants on retinal morphology are not fully understood. We did not have detailed information on medication duration and dosage for all patients.

The lack of correlation between retinal changes and depression severity in most parameters, combined with the unexpected positive correlation in some regions, complicates the interpretation of our findings. These inconsistencies may reflect measurement variability, true biological heterogeneity, or limitations of the PHQ-9 as a measure of depression severity.

Finally, the diagnostic utility of OCT parameters was limited, suggesting that retinal imaging alone is insufficient for clinical diagnosis of depression.

## 4.8 Future Directions

Several directions for future research emerge from our findings:

**Longitudinal studies** are essential to determine the temporal dynamics of retinal changes, their relationship to depression course and treatment response, and whether they represent trait or state markers (Rush et al., 2006; Harmer et al., 2017).

**Mechanistic studies** using animal models could elucidate the biological pathways linking depression and retinal pathology, including the role of neuroinflammation, neurotrophic factors, and autonomic dysfunction.

**Multi-modal biomarker research** combining OCT with other measures (neuroimaging, peripheral inflammatory markers, genetic risk scores) may achieve better diagnostic and prognostic performance than any single modality.

**Intervention studies** could determine whether successful antidepressant treatment normalizes retinal structure, which would support the utility of OCT as a treatment monitoring tool.

**Subtype-specific analyses** may reveal distinct patterns of retinal changes associated with specific depression subtypes (e.g., melancholic vs. atypical, inflammatory vs. non-inflammatory).

# 5. Conclusion

This study represents the first comprehensive systematic investigation of retinal structural changes in first-episode, drug-naïve MDD patients, examining both macular (five-layer analysis) and optic disc parameters using OCT. This methodological approach eliminates confounding from antidepressant medications and chronic disease course, providing uncontaminated biological insights into depression's ocular manifestations. Our findings demonstrate that MDD is associated with systematic structural changes in the retina, characterized by reduced macular thickness (particularly in the outer temporal region) and optic disc alterations requiring further ophthalmologic evaluation. These associations remain significant after controlling for demographic confounders, supporting an independent relationship between depression and retinal pathology. However, the modest effect sizes and limited discriminatory performance suggest that OCT parameters are not suitable as standalone diagnostic biomarkers. The unexpected positive correlation between depression severity and retinal thickness in certain regions challenges simple neurodegenerative models and may reflect complex interactions between inflammatory and compensatory processes. Longitudinal studies are critically needed to determine whether retinal thinning predicts treatment response, whether it normalizes with symptom improvement, and whether it represents a stable trait marker or a dynamic state marker of MDD.
