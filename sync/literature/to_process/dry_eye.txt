Purpose: The aim of this real-world registry study was to evaluate the quality-of-life (QoL) impact of dry eye
disease (DED). The specific objectives were to determine factors affecting QoL in DED, and to evaluate the
psychometric properties of the Ocular Surface Disease Index (OSDI), Ocular Comfort Index (OCI), and Patient
Health Questionnaire (PHQ) in a real-world DED population using modern psychometric methods.
Methods: This descriptive cross-sectional study included 368 DED patients (mean 54.7 ± 16.6 years; 80.2 %
female) who completed one or more of the three questionnaires (OSDI, OCI and PHQ). Psychometric analysis of
the QoL data was carried out with Andrich’s Rating Scale Model of Rasch analysis.
Results: The original OSDI and OCI had disordered categories. The Rasch-optimised final QoL scales (OSDI –
Overall, OSDI – Symptoms [SY], OSDI – Activity limitation [AL], OSDI – Environmental trigger [EN], OCI
Overall, OCI - Frequency [FR], OCI - Intensity [IN], and PHQ) had satisfactory psychometric properties. Patients
diagnosed with a mixed aqueous/evaporative DED subtype had worse mean OSDI-Overall and OSDI-AL scores
than individuals with evaporative DED (p = 0.012 and 0.001 respectively). Patients with corneal neuropathic
pain had worse QoL scores (OSDI-Overall, OSDI-AL, OSDI-SY, OSDI-EN, OCI-Overall, OCI-FR, OCI-IN, and PHQ;
all p < 0.05) than those without. Patients who reported undergoing prior treatment or procedure for DED had
worse QoL scores than those who did not (all p < 0.05 except for PHQ). The associations between DED signs (tear
breakup time and staining) and symptoms were weak or not significant.
Conclusions: In this real-world setting, diagnoses of mixed DED, corneal neuropathic pain, and history of DED
treatment/procedures were associated with worse dry eye symptoms, activity limitation, and/or QoL.

Most of the existing QoL studies in DED have used traditional
analytical techniques, such as summary scoring [1–3], which have
methodological limitations. The summary scoring erroneously assigns
each item in a scale the same weighting, with response categories
located equidistant from adjacent categories [26–28]. Modern psychometric methods, such as Rasch analysis, can address these shortcomings
[29,30]. This real-world clinical registry study sought to comprehensively evaluate the impact of DED on QoL. The specific objectives were
to determine the factors affecting QoL, and to evaluate the psychometric
properties of the OSDI, OCI, and PHQ-4 in a real-world DED population,
using Rasch analysis.

Dry eye disease (DED) is one of the most common ocular conditions
worldwide [1–3]. It results from reduced quality and/or quantity of
tears, leading to compromise of the ocular surface [4]. Globally, DED
causes significant morbidity [5] and healthcare expenditure [1,4]. The
prevalence of DED varies around the world, with estimates ranging from
5 % to 50 % of adults depending on the geographic location and definition(s) applied [6]. The burden of DED is growing due to lifestyle
changes (e.g., prolonged screen use) and the ageing population [2,7,8].
The subjective impact of DED can range from mild ocular discomfort
to disabling eye pain [9,10]. Severe limitations on daily activity are less
common but can include difficulties with driving and reading [4]. Most
studies evaluating patient-reported outcomes among people living with
DED have focused on ocular symptoms; the impacts of DED on other
quality of life (QoL) domains are less well understood. Comprehensively
evaluating the QoL impact of DED, from a patient’s perspective, is
important as standard ophthalmic clinical measures, such as visual
acuity (VA), tear breakup time (TBUT) and ocular surface staining, may
not reflect the impact of the disease on QoL and/or real-world visual
function [1]. For example, a person with DED may have normal (6/6)
high-contrast visual acuity, but may not feel comfortable safely driving
at night [11].
It has been reported that clinicians frequently underestimate the
severity of DED relative to patients’ self-assessment of their disease
severity [2]. Moreover, some individuals with DED may suffer from
corneal neuropathic pain, manifesting with few standard ocular surface
signs in the presence of severe eye pain [10]. Real-world data have
provided useful insight into the impact of a variety of ocular diseases,
and their management, on QoL outcomes [12,13]. Unlike clinical trials
that are conducted among discrete populations in controlled settings,
studies that analyse routinely-collected clinical outcome data can provide real-world insights. Clinical registries have been used to collect
short- and longer-term real-world data from different patient populations [14–17]. For DED, the Save Sight Dry Eye Registry (SSDER) is a
web-based, multinational patient database for monitoring natural history and treatment outcomes, including clinical and patient-reported
QoL outcomes [18–20]. Analysing data entered into the SSDER can
assist with determining the long-term effectiveness and safety of DED
treatments, identifying patients at a higher risk of developing severe
DED, and developing individually tailored treatment regimens to
enhance person-centred care [18,21].
The Ocular Surface Disease Index (OSDI), Ocular Comfort Index
(OCI), and Patient Health Questionnaire-4 (PHQ-4) are the three measures used in the SSDER [18] to collect real-world patient-reported
outcome data by clinicians. These patient-reported outcome measures
were chosen after a literature review and international steering committee consensus based on how frequently they are used, and evidence
on validity, reliability, and psychometric properties, with a goal of
capturing the comprehensive impact of DED on patients with minimal
data entry burden to patients and clinicians. The OSDI [22], the most
frequently used DED-specific patient-reported outcome measure globally, has three subscales: symptoms (OSDI-SY; consists of both visual and
ocular comfort symptoms), vision-related activity limitation (OSDI-AL),
and environmental triggers (OSDI-EN). OSDI consists of 3 types of
4-point Likert scales, traditionally scored with summary scoring. The
OCI [23] has been reported to be of higher psychometric quality than
other DED questionnaires [24]. However, it has content on only the
ocular comfort symptoms domain of QoL. It evaluates the frequency
(OCI-FR) and intensity (OCI-IN) attributes of six symptoms. The mental

2. Materials and methods
This cross-sectional study included patients with a diagnosis of DED,
in one or both eyes, who completed one or more of the three patientreported outcome measures: OSDI, OCI and PHQ. They were recruited
from six practices (eight clinicians) within Australia, Spain, and Nepal,
with data collected prospectively using the SSDER [18] from April 2020
to April 2024. The SSDER incorporates quality assurance measures,
including mandatory fields and the requirement that questionnaires be
completed before data can be finalised [18]. Only finalised data were
analysed in this study.
Ethical approval was granted by the Human Research Ethics Committees of the Royal Australian and New Zealand College of Ophthalmologists (RANZCO), the Royal Prince Alfred Hospital, and locally for
other participating sites. The study was conducted in adherence with the
tenets of the Declaration of Helsinki.
DED and corneal neuropathic pain diagnoses and treatment decisions were made by each individual patient’s treating clinician,
reflecting the nature of real-world clinical practice. Data recorded in the
SSDER [18] included patient demographics, medical history (including
DED treatment details) and clinical measures, including visual acuity
(VA), TBUT, ocular surface staining (none, minimal, mild, moderate,
severe) and Oxford ocular surface staining score (OSS, 0–15).
Patient-reported outcomes were captured in the registry using the OSDI
[31,32], OCI [33], and PHQ-4 [34,35] questionnaires (Table 1).
2.1. Rasch analysis
Psychometric evaluation of the QoL data was carried out using Rasch
analysis with Winsteps software (version 3.92.1; Winsteps, Chicago, IL)
employing Andrich’s Rating Scale Model. Details of the Rasch analysis
have been described previously [13,26,27,36]. In brief, Rasch analysis
converts raw categorical patient-reported outcome data into
interval-level data with log transformation. The data were coded such
that the higher scores represented better QoL (e.g., fewer symptoms and
less activity limitation). Rasch iterations were conducted until acceptable values of Rasch parameters were obtained to ensure that the data
met the Rasch model expectations.
Rasch parameters evaluated included response-category functioning,
unidimensionality, measurement precision, fit statistics, differential
item functioning, local item dependency (LID), and targeting [26].
Response category functioning assessment ensured that the response
options were used as intended with higher categories representing better
QoL status. Unidimensionality evaluation ensured that each scale
measured a single underlying construct (e.g., activity limitation) which
is a fundamental property of a scale. In this study, unidimensionality
was assessed with variance explained by the measure and unexplained

3. Results
3.1. General characteristics of the participants
This study included data from 368 patients (mean ± SD, 54.7 ± 16.6
years) enrolled in the SSDER, with a clinician diagnosis of DED and who
had completed one or more of the three patient-reported outcome
measures: OSDI, OCI and PHQ. The patient data were derived from six
practices (eight clinicians; 6 ophthalmologists and 2 optometrists) in
Australia, Spain and Nepal. A total of 266 (72.3 %) patients had
completed all three questionnaires, 338 (91.8 %) patients had
completed both the OSDI and PHQ, 296 (80.4 %) had completed OCI.
Tear breakup time (TBUT) was assessed using fluorescein in 301 (81.8
%) patients, and using non-invasive methods in 67 (18.2 %) patients.
The key demographic and clinical characteristics of included patients
are summarised in Table 2. According to the clinician-reported subtype
of DED, 120 (32.6 %) patients had mixed DED (i.e., both aqueousdeficient and evaporative subtypes), 206 (56.0 %) patients had only
evaporative DED, and 42 (11.4 %) patients had only aqueous-deficient
DED. Forty (10.9 %) patients received a diagnosis of corneal neuropathic pain. Twenty-three patients (6.2 %) were contact lens wearers.

Our findings from the multinational real-world registry setting
showed that the OSDI, the most frequently used dry eye questionnaire
worldwide, should ideally not be used in its raw form aligning with and
validating findings from previous research [32,39]. It is essential to
assess its validity and optimise it before using it as an outcome measure.
In our study and that of Dougherty [32], the OSDI was modified to
achieve acceptable psychometric properties. However, in a study by
McAlinden et al., the OSDI could not be repaired [39]. The validity of a
QoL measure can change over time and across different settings. While
studies using traditional methods suggest that the OSDI is valid and
reliable [31], approaches that use summary scoring have limitations
[45]. Summary scoring has been described as ‘malpractice’ in QoL
research [46], such that the use of modern psychometric methods such
as Rasch analysis is now considered best practice.
The known-group validity of the QoL scales was evident from their
ability to differentiate various clinical DED groups [43]. In this current
real-world study, evaporative DED was the most common subtype, as
reported elsewhere [47]. Patients with mixed DED (i.e., coexistent
evaporative and aqueous-deficient forms) had worse QoL scores than
those with only evaporative DED or only aqueous deficient DED. It
should be noted that the mixed DED may represent a more advanced
stage of DED, hence associated with worse QoL [48]. We also found that
a prior history of a DED treatment or procedure was associated with
worse QoL scores. While appropriate treatment may improve patients’
QoL [2], we would expect those cases to be more likely to have chronic
DED, and perhaps more severe DED than others. Interestingly, the TBUT
were similar between the groups. The findings, at least in this clinical
population, also suggest that current DED treatments may not improve
QoL to the level experienced by people who have never had DED. Patients with a clinical diagnosis of corneal neuropathic pain had worse
symptoms, activity limitation, mental health status and QoL than those
without such a diagnosis. The impact of moderate or severe DED on QoL
has been reported as similar to that of angina by Schiffman et al[49].
The larger proportion of females than males in this study is consistent
with the higher prevalence of DED in this population, as reported elsewhere [2,50]. Some of the reasons that have been described to underlie a
higher burden of DED in females are that they are more likely to report
symptoms, undergo refractive surgery, wear contact lenses, have a
higher incidence of autoimmune disease, take more medications, and
have hormonal differences [4]. Interestingly, we found that the differences in QoL impacts of DED between males and females were not statistically significant in the present study. This suggests that while
females may be more likely to receive a DED diagnosis, the QoL impacts
are comparable for both sexes once diagnosed.
The weak or non-significant correlations observed between DED
symptoms and clinical signs may be because of decreased corneal
sensation in chronic DED [1], or reflect individual differences in
discomfort thresholds and coping mechanisms [2]. Some patients with
mild signs may report severe symptoms, whereas others with severe
signs can report mild or no symptoms [1,50]. The relationship between
patient-reported outcomes and clinical DED signs is reported to be
largely weak or non-existent in the literature [1–3,37,50]. It follows that
DED signs and symptoms are complementary clinical measures, and thus
should both be assessed as part of a comprehensive clinical evaluation of
DED. Corneal neuropathic pain is being increasingly recognised and may
often be misdiagnosed as DED [51]. Questionnaires designed for corneal
neuropathic pain may be needed to more clearly identify this patient

4. Discussion
This international DED registry study has comprehensively evaluated the QoL impact of DED using seven scales within three patientreported outcome measures. The Rasch-optimised QoL scales had
satisfactory psychometric properties and were used to identify the factors associated with decreased QoL in DED patients. The presence of
mixed subtype DED, corneal neuropathic pain, a prior history of DED
treatment, and a prior history of a procedure for DED were associated
with worse DED symptoms, activity limitation, and/or reduced QoL.
Associations between DED signs and symptoms were weak or nonsignificant.
Using Rasch analysis, as a modern psychometric method based on
robust measurement principles, allowed us to define the impact of DED
on ocular symptoms, activity limitations and QoL, and their relationship
with typical clinical DED signs. The OSDI and OCI scales were optimised
by collapsing the disordered categories, similar to a study by Dougherty
et al. [32]; the optimised QoL scales had overall satisfactory psychometric properties. Significant correlations between the OSDI and OCI
scales demonstrated the convergent validity of these measures [43].
Vision-related activity limitation (OSDI-AL) significantly correlated
with symptom domains (OSDI, OSDI-SY, OSDI-EN, OCI, OCI-FR, and
OCI-SY), as reported by Tong et al. [44] Ocular surface grittiness and
pain were two items present in the OSDI-SY, OCI-FR and OCI-IN questionnaires, although with slightly different wording. In all cases, ‘grittiness’ was rated as more troublesome than the ‘pain’ item by patients
with DED, demonstrating the measurement invariance property of the
scales. Rasch analysis results improved understanding of the impact of

the specific importance of evaluating patient-reported outcomes as DED
symptoms were more influential on the mental health indices of DED
patients than other factors such as ocular surface signs, visual acuity, or
patient age.
Lower visual acuity was associated with activity limitation. This is
expected as reduced visual acuity can affect reading, driving, working
on a computer and/or watching TV; the factors captured by the OSDI-AL
scale. Visual acuity was not correlated with other QoL scale scores,
TBUT or staining. Patients with an unstable tear film, leading to a short
TBUT and ocular surface staining, may report intermittent blurred vision
and glare [1], but can achieve visual acuity test results within normative
ranges, especially when increased blinking and an extended viewing
time are encouraged. Functional visual acuity, assessed by
spatial-contrast sensitivity and reading speed, has been reported to be
reduced in DED patients [1], and thus may be more useful for quantifying the visual impact of DED.
Several factors should be considered when interpreting the findings
from the present study. First, although a comprehensive approach was
taken to investigate the QoL impact of DED, QoL domains such as economic well-being were not directly evaluated. A significant negative
correlation between OSDI scores and work productivity has been reported [53]. DED can lead to high direct (e.g., cost of treatments) and
indirect (e.g., loss of productivity) economic costs [2]. Second, clinical
results for tests such as tear osmolarity, tear film interferometry, and
topographic measures (e.g., surface regularity index and surface asymmetry index [1]) were not included in this analysis as these are often not
routinely available in clinical practice. Third, modern psychometric
methods such as Rasch analysis are rarely used in DED studies (unlike in
other areas of ophthalmology [54]), which makes it challenging to
compare findings of the current study with those of other studies.
Fourth, QoL is a complex multidimensional construct confounded by
several factors, such as patient expectations, coping mechanisms, health
care service availability and quality, socio-economic conditions, and
environment. Fifth, there might have been variations in clinical practice
by clinicians and practices including DED diagnosis criteria which is
another limitation. Diagnoses of DED and corneal neuropathic pain were
made by the contributing clinicians, reflecting real-world practice.
There are no clear diagnostic criteria by which ocular neuropathic pain
can be assessed, and it’s often a diagnosis of exclusion [51]. The current
study thus highlights current general trends in the clinical care of DED
patients. Defining the specific roles of individual treatments [2] was
beyond the scope of the present work. Future longitudinal studies using
the SSDER [18] will be valuable for advancing understanding of the
real-world effectiveness of DED treatments, including from the patient’s
perspective. While the present findings improve our understanding of
the factors associated with worse QoL, as is the case for other observational studies, this analysis cannot establish causality.
The QoL scores between the DED subgroups should be interpreted
with caution, considering both statistical and clinical (MCID) significances. It is important to note that statistical significance does not
necessarily imply clinical significance, and the reverse is also true. We
calculated distribution-based MCID values to interpret the QoL scores as
it was not possible to determine anchor-based MCIDs due to lack of a
gold-standard anchor [40]. The distribution-based MCID approach is
particularly suitable in DED, as studies often report a lack of correlation
between signs and symptoms in DED. However, the distribution-based
MCID values based on half the standard deviation are highly

dependent on the characteristics of the study population. The combination of anchor-based methods and standard error could provide more
robust MCIDs.
This is the first original research publication from the SSDER, and
shows the utility of this international, dry eye registry for understanding
healthcare outcomes in DED. Appropriate measurement of patient outcomes is the first step to being able to quantify meaningful improvements in health outcomes. The use of Rasch analysis, a modern
psychometric method based on robust measurement principles, was a
major strength of the study. Using this approach, we were able to obtain
interval-level data from the raw categorical data of multiple patientreported outcome tools to conduct quantitative analyses allowing
those most at risk of QoL impact to be identified. The presence of mixed
subtype DED, corneal neuropathic pain, and prior history of a DED
treatment or procedure were associated with worse symptoms, activity
limitation, and/or QoL. The results also indicated that DED clinical signs
and symptoms may not correlate in patients with DED even when using
robust tools and methods. These findings highlight the importance of
adopting an individualised approach to DED management, as predicting
symptoms from signs (or vice versa) may not be possible with current
approaches. A comprehensive dry eye assessment should include the
evaluation of both clinical signs and symptoms, to capture complementary information that informs DED management.
